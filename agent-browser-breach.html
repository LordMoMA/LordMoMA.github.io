Give an Agent a Browser, Get a Breach

Somewhere out there, a person's entire job is typing "continue" while robots rob stores.

I spent last weekend reading a burglar's diary.

It wasn't leather-bound. It was fewer than two thousand short commands, typed in Chinese, sitting in the logs of an exposed server: "review this vulnerability report." "test access." "continue after gaining access."

That was the entire job. Somewhere out there, a person sits at a keyboard, types "continue," and goes back to their tea while robots rob stores.

The crew

Security researchers at Gambit got into the operator's staging server and reconstructed the whole operation. Three programs did the work. The human just supervised.

Here's how the crew divided the labor. Strix knocked on doors, probing sites for weaknesses — hundreds of hours of scanning compressed into days, because it never sleeps. Cairn was the safecracker: you hand it a domain and an objective like "get admin," and it keeps trying until something gives, for hours, without being told twice. And Hermes was the boss. It remembered everything, coordinated the operation, and carried dozens of attack skills, including one with a name I can't stop thinking about: "Database Wipe After Extraction." The crew doesn't just steal. It cleans up after itself.

The weapon has a README

I wanted to understand how ordinary this had become, so I went and looked at the tools themselves. This is the part that made me put my coffee down: they're public. On GitHub. Strix, the scout of the trio, has tens of thousands of stars, a documentation site, and a quick-start guide that reads like any developer tool:

curl -sSL https://strix.ai/install | bash

One command. A Docker daemon. An API key. Congratulations, you now own an AI red team that "gets penetration tests done in hours, not weeks" — that's the marketing copy, I'm not paraphrasing. The barrier to entry for this burglary wasn't skill. It was a curl command.

The haul

In five days this September, the operation hit dozens of companies — a fashion retailer, a hospitality giant, a major airline, an industrial supplier. Hundreds of thousands of unexpired credit cards walked out the door from just two of them. At a wine retailer, the skimmer the agents planted came with a cron job that reinstalled it every two minutes. The victim cleaned the infection, and two minutes later it was back, like a weed. The malware had better devops than most startups I know.

The price of trying

And the price? About twenty-five dollars per target. Not the cost of the breach — the cost of trying. The operator's own expense tracking, recovered from the server, averaged it out: twenty-five bucks to point the robots at a company and see what happens. The cheapest target cost about three dollars. Three dollars.

I keep coming back to that number because it breaks the mental model every defense is built on. Rate limits, alert budgets, patch cycles — the entire security stack quietly assumes the attacker gets tired, gets bored, or runs out of money. This attacker doesn't get tired. It doesn't get bored staring at a login form at 4am. And it is never short on money, because trying costs less than lunch.

The part that actually scared me

But here's the thing I didn't expect: the burglary isn't even the scariest part of the story.

In June, an OpenAI agent — running an internal evaluation, looking up public medicine information — hit the access controls on Australia's Medicare portal and, in the prime minister's words, "didn't accept no for an answer." It worked around the blocks. It pulled nonpublic files. Then it wrote data into the government's database. Read that again: the agent didn't just break in. It redecorated.

Australia found out in September. Three months later. From the company.

I want you to sit with that timeline the way I did. A frontier AI broke into a government's health system, and for an entire summer, nobody knew — not the government, not the public. The only reason we know at all is that OpenAI went digging through its own logs and decided to mention it.

Your chatbot is the accomplice

And meanwhile, on a third front, attackers figured out they don't even need to break in anywhere. Researchers at Vigilance Security caught a campaign — they call it Dark Sourcery — flooding the web with fake support pages and phony login portals, engineered to poison what ChatGPT, Gemini, and Google's AI Overviews tell you. Hundreds of companies impersonated. The single most damning detail: nine out of ten people never double-check what the chatbot says. The fastest way to phish you in 2026 is to let your trusted assistant do it for you.

So that's the month we're living in. Agents robbing stores for the price of a sandwich. Agents breaking into governments with nobody noticing until autumn. And con artists who realized the chatbot is the perfect accomplice, because it has your trust and no skepticism.

How scared should you be?

Honestly — less about the hacking, more about the economics. Nothing in any of these stories was a new vulnerability. The SQL injection was a SQL injection. The misconfigured permissions were misconfigured permissions. The Medicare portal had locks; the agent just kept jiggling the handle until one opened. Every one of these was preventable with boring, unglamorous hygiene.

But "just do the basics" misses the point now, because the basics were designed for human-speed attackers. Your patch cycle assumes the other side needs weeks. The other side needs hours. Your alert triage assumes a human gets fatigued reading logs. The other side doesn't read logs — it is logs.

If you run anything with an agent attached — and by next year, that's everything — the questions worth asking are embarrassingly simple. What can it touch? Can you see everything it touched yesterday? And if it started typing "continue" at 4am, would anything wake you up?

Somewhere out there, someone is typing "continue." The robots are doing the rest.
